Forecasting the 2026 U.S. Holiday Retail Season

Classical, machine-learning and AI-chatbot forecasters compared - and a planning tool for small retailers

UCLA Anderson School of Management · Time Series Analysis and Forecasting · Dr. William Yu
Group project, October 2026 · Charlotte Zhu, Shihan Wang, Jing Chang, Rachel Liu
Interactive dashboard: https://holiday-retail-planner.streamlit.app
Code and data: https://github.com/RachelLiu13/holiday-retail-forecast

Executive summary

November and December generate about 18% of U.S. retail sales (22% for clothing stores), yet retailers must commit to holiday inventory in late summer. Small retailers typically plan on 'same as last year'. Using U.S. Census monthly retail sales by category (1992 to August 2026), we tested seven statistical and machine-learning forecasters and two AI chatbots on 11 past holiday seasons, each time forecasting from the end of August with only the data available then. We then forecast the 2026 season and built an interactive dashboard that turns the forecast into an order recommendation for a small store.

  • 2026 outlook: our ensemble forecasts Nov+Dec total retail sales of $1,383.1B, +5.2% over 2025. E-commerce leads (+9.6%); grocery is weakest (+1.1%). The first month already published after our forecast date (September 2026, total retail) came in within 1.7% of the forecast.
  • Forecasting beats habit: 'same as last year' missed the holiday total by 5.3% on average; our ensemble (SARIMA + ETS + XGBoost) missed by 2.3% and beat it in 77% of category-seasons.
  • Consumer sentiment does not improve the forecast (error change +0.02 points). How shoppers say they feel and what they spend have diverged.
  • AI chatbots - hindsight, not foresight: Told the category and the year, Claude looked like the best forecaster of all (2.0% average error). With them hidden, its error rose to 2.5%. The named prompt won in 65% of 77 category-seasons (p = 0.002, statistically significant), and the gap was largest in 2020-2022 (+1.2 points vs +0.5 overall) - exactly when knowing what really happened (the pandemic, inflation) helps most. That is hindsight, not forecasting, so only anonymized scores measure skill.
  • Fairly tested, chatbots are good but not better: On anonymized data, the chatbots missed the holiday total by Claude 2.5%, GPT 2.9%, vs 2.3% for our ensemble and 5.3% for 'same as last year'; the ensemble was better than Claude in 57% of cases (p = 0.08, not statistically significant); the ensemble was better than GPT in 61% of cases (p = 0.004, statistically significant).
  • Product: a public dashboard with the forecast, a full model comparison, a Holiday Planner that sizes a store's order and safety stock, and an AI advisor that explains the results in plain English using only computed numbers.

Recommendation for retailers: plan the holiday season on a statistical forecast rather than last year's numbers, size safety stock from measured forecast error, and treat AI-chatbot forecasts with caution: they can look excellent on the past for the wrong reason.

1. Motivation and research questions

The holiday season is retail's most important and least forgiving period. Inventory is ordered months in advance; under-ordering means empty shelves during the busiest weeks, over-ordering means January markdowns. Large chains employ forecasting teams; small and independent retailers - who often lack such resources - commonly plan by copying last year. Meanwhile, generative AI tools such as ChatGPT and Claude are increasingly asked for business forecasts, though it is unclear whether they forecast or merely recall published data. We address five questions:

  • Q1. How much will Americans spend in the 2026 holiday season (November-December), by category?
  • Q2. Do forecasting models beat 'same as last year', and which model is most accurate?
  • Q3. Can large language models forecast as well as statistical models, once memorization is ruled out?
  • Q4. Does a customer-side indicator - consumer sentiment - improve the forecast?
  • Q5. How can a small retailer turn a national category forecast into an order quantity?

Our deliverables use AI in both ways suggested by the course: Option A, LLMs as forecasters compared with conventional models under identical out-of-sample conditions; and Option B, an interactive dashboard with an AI advisor.

3.2 Out-of-sample design

We use a rolling-origin backtest that mimics a real planning decision. For each year 2015-2025, every model stands at the end of August, is estimated only on data through August (SARIMA orders are re-selected each time), and forecasts September-December (horizons 1-4). All models share the same origin, horizon and information set. The sentiment variable is lagged four months, so every value a forecast needs is already published at the origin. This gives 7 categories × 11 seasons = 77 holiday forecasts per model. The identical code then runs from August 2026 for the live forecast.

3.3 Accuracy measures

  • Holiday APE (main metric): |forecast − actual| / actual for the November+December total - the number a retailer orders against.
  • MAPE, MAE, RMSE over the four forecast months. MAPE is appropriate because retail sales are never near zero.
  • MASE: MAE divided by the in-sample seasonal-naive MAE (training data only), comparable across categories of very different size.
  • Coverage80: share of actual values inside a model's 80% prediction interval (ETS and SARIMA), to check whether the uncertainty is honest.
  • Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the 77 matched category-seasons to judge whether differences between two models are significant.

3.4 LLM forecasting protocol and the memorization problem

LLMs are trained on large web corpora that very likely include the published Census figures and news about past holiday seasons. A backtest that names the series and year could reward recall, not forecasting. We therefore ran two framings. Anonymized (main test, both LLMs): 120 months rescaled to an index (first month = 100), labelled only by calendar month - no category, no year, no units. Named (Claude only): the same history with real dates, the category name and dollar values. If named prompts are systematically more accurate on past seasons, the difference measures hindsight. Both LLMs received the same system instruction ('use only the numbers provided ... forecast the next 4 months') and returned JSON with a short explanation; Claude ran at high reasoning effort with schema-constrained output, GPT-5.5 with JSON mode. All prompts and responses are stored in the repository for audit. For 2026 nothing can be memorized, so the named framing is used. An excerpt of an anonymized prompt (clothing, August 2024):

3.5 From forecast to order

The Holiday Planner converts the national forecast into a store-level order. Expected sales = the store's own Nov+Dec 2025 sales × (1 + forecast category growth + an optional store adjustment). Uncertainty σ = √(σnational² + σstore²), where σnational is the root-mean-square of the ensemble's past holiday % errors for that category (excluding COVID seasons) and σstore is the owner's estimate of how far the store usually misses plan; the two are independent, so variances add. Safety stock = z × σ × expected sales for a chosen service level, and the recommended order = max(0, expected + safety stock − on hand − on order). Conservative and optimistic scenarios use ±1.28σ (10th/90th percentiles).

Every model clearly beats 'same as last year' (5.32%): the ensemble cuts the holiday error by more than half to 2.29%. Among classical/ML models, SARIMA (2.34%) and the ensemble lead, while the simple 'last year × growth' rule (2.55%) is surprisingly competitive - the key improvement is accounting for the current trend at all. Differences of a few tenths of a point among the top models are within the noise of an 11-season sample, so having a model matters more than which model. XGBoost has the highest error among the learned models, plausibly because roughly 25 years of monthly data is a small sample for machine learning; pooling categories helps but does not overcome this.

4.2 Which categories are hard to forecast?

Total retail, grocery and general merchandise are easiest (errors near 1-2%): they are large, diversified and stable. Electronics, sporting/hobby and e-commerce are hardest: they depend on product cycles (new consoles and phones), shifting promotion calendars, and - for e-commerce - a structural trend that changed abruptly in 2020. The table shows the same ranking for every model, so difficulty is a property of the category, not the method.

4.3 Over time and COVID

Seasonal Naive fails badly in 2020 and 2021 because last year's pattern was no guide during and right after the pandemic; trend-aware models adapt. Excluding those two seasons narrows but does not close the gap (3.78% vs 2.15%). 2018 was the hardest season for every model.

4.4 Are the prediction intervals honest?

SARIMA's nominal 80% intervals contained 83% of actual values and ETS's 82% - close to 80%, so the stated uncertainty can be trusted. This is why the planner sizes safety stock from measured forecast errors rather than from a guess, and why the dashboard only shows intervals for models that produce a statistically valid one.

4.5 Does consumer sentiment help? (Q4)

No. Adding lagged consumer sentiment to SARIMA changed the average holiday error by +0.02 points (2.36% vs 2.34%). Sentiment has been historically weak through 2025-2026 (down 10-30% year over year) while nominal spending kept growing 3-8%. What consumers say about their mood is a poor guide to what they spend - at least once trend and seasonality are already modelled.

Told the category and the year, Claude looked like the best forecaster of all (2.0% average error). With them hidden, its error rose to 2.5%. The named prompt won in 65% of 77 category-seasons (p = 0.002, statistically significant), and the gap was largest in 2020-2022 (+1.2 points vs +0.5 overall) - exactly when knowing what really happened (the pandemic, inflation) helps most. That is hindsight, not forecasting, so only anonymized scores measure skill.

On anonymized data, the chatbots missed the holiday total by Claude 2.5%, GPT 2.9%, vs 2.3% for our ensemble and 5.3% for 'same as last year'; the ensemble was better than Claude in 57% of cases (p = 0.08, not statistically significant); the ensemble was better than GPT in 61% of cases (p = 0.004, statistically significant).

Reading the models' own explanations (saved with every response) shows how the LLMs work: they compute last year's ratios of November and December to the late-summer level and apply them to the current level - essentially a careful 'seasonal ratio' method. That is sensible and explains why they beat 'same as last year', but it does not use the information a statistical model extracts from 25 years of history, and it gives no calibrated uncertainty. The practical lesson: an LLM that is told what and when it is forecasting can look better than any model on past data, for the wrong reason. Backtests of LLM forecasts must hide information that can be recalled.

4.7 What drives the machine-learning model?

The most important XGBoost inputs are the growth rates: last year's growth for the same target month (which captures shifts in the holiday calendar, such as a late Thanksgiving moving sales between November and December), the latest year-over-year growth and its 12-month average. Consumer sentiment carries little weight, consistent with Section 4.5. In other words, the model learns what the statistical models assume - that this year's holiday season is last year's pattern scaled by the current trend - and it adds little beyond that with only about 25 years of data per category.

6. The product: Holiday Retail Planner dashboard (Option B)

The dashboard (https://holiday-retail-planner.streamlit.app) is built with Streamlit and reads the same saved outputs as this report - nothing is re-estimated in the app, so what users see is exactly what the code produced. It has seven tabs:

  • Story - motivation and headline findings.
  • Market - history by category, the size of each December spike, e-commerce share.
  • 2026 Forecast - choose a category and any forecasters; SARIMA's 80% interval as a band; the LLMs' own explanations.
  • Model Battle - all backtest metrics with or without COVID seasons, the category heatmap, a replay of any past season, and the LLM memorization test.
  • Holiday Planner - the order calculator of Section 3.5 with scenario table and November/December timing.
  • AI Advisor - a chat assistant (Claude) that answers questions such as 'Why do you recommend this order?' or 'Which model is most accurate?'. It receives a fact sheet generated from the results plus the user's planner inputs, is instructed to quote numbers exactly and never invent forecasts or ranges, and says when the information is missing.
  • About - objectives, data, methods, validation, usage instructions and AI-tool disclosure.

Worked example. A clothing boutique sold 5,000 units in Nov+Dec 2025, has 1,200 on hand, wants a 90% chance of not running out and estimates its sales usually miss plan by ±10%. National clothing growth is +2.9%, so expected demand is 5,144 units. The ensemble's past clothing error is 1.2%; combined with the store's ±10%, σ = 10.1%, giving safety stock of 664 units and a recommended order of 4,609 units. Note that store-level volatility, not national forecast error, dominates the buffer - an honest message for small retailers.

7. Conclusions

  • Use a model, not last year. Any trend-aware forecast halves the holiday planning error; the ensemble is the most accurate and most consistent choice.
  • Sentiment surveys add nothing beyond trend and seasonality for nominal holiday spending.
  • Be careful with AI-chatbot forecasts. They can look better than any model on past data because they remember the past; tested fairly they are reasonable but not better than standard methods, and they provide no calibrated uncertainty. Their best role is explaining forecasts - which is how our AI advisor uses them.
  • 2026 should be a good holiday season (+5.2%), with e-commerce continuing to take share.
  • Forecast error is measurable and should drive safety stock - and for a small store, its own volatility matters more than the national forecast error.

8. Limitations

  • National category totals, not a specific store or product; the planner scales a store's own history by national growth.
  • FRED provides today's revised data. Forecasters in past years saw earlier, unrevised figures, so real-time accuracy would be somewhat lower for all models.
  • Eleven seasons per category is a small sample; differences of a few tenths of a point between the best models are not decisive, which is why we report paired significance tests.
  • LLM outputs are not deterministic, and each prompt was run once; model versions will change. Anonymization removes direct recall but an LLM might still recognize a series' shape (e.g. the 2020 shock).
  • No inventory-cost or stock-out data, so we make no claim about money saved; growth figures are nominal.
